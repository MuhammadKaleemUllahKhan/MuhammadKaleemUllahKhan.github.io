Adversarial ML

Adversarial Examples and the Long Road to Robust Models

April 18, 2026 8 min read Muhammad KaleemUllah Khan

Take an image a neural network classifies correctly with 99% confidence. Nudge a few pixels by an amount too small for your eye to notice. The same network now insists, just as confidently, that it is looking at something else entirely. That unsettling gap between human and machine perception is the world of adversarial examples, and it is one of the most revealing problems in modern machine learning.

Adversarial robustness sits at the center of my PhD, so I have a lot of affection for how cleanly this problem exposes what our models really learned versus what we hoped they learned. Let me unpack how the attacks work, how we defend against them, and why the defense is never free.

What is an adversarial example?

An adversarial example is an input that has been deliberately perturbed to fool a model, while staying almost identical to a normal input. The perturbation is tiny, often bounded so that no single pixel changes by more than a sliver, yet it is aimed with surgical precision. The model does not see noise; it sees, from its own warped vantage point, a different class.

The reason this is possible is that high-dimensional decision boundaries are far more fragile than they look. A model can be accurate on natural data and still have class boundaries that run startlingly close to almost every point. The attacker's whole job is to find the shortest shove across that nearby boundary.

How attacks are built

The elegant, slightly uncomfortable insight is that the same gradient we use to train a model can be used to attack it. During training we compute how the loss changes with respect to the weights, and step the weights to reduce it. To attack, we compute how the loss changes with respect to the input, and step the input to increase it.

One step: FGSM

The fast gradient sign method is the one-line version: take the sign of the input gradient and move every pixel a small fixed amount in the direction that hurts the model most. 

x_adv = x + epsilon * sign( grad_x  loss(model(x), y) )

epsilon is the budget, how much we allow ourselves to change the input. Small epsilon, imperceptible change; larger epsilon, stronger but more visible attack.

Many steps: PGD

Projected gradient descent is FGSM applied repeatedly in small steps, projecting back into the allowed budget after each one. By iterating, it finds much more effective perturbations and is widely treated as a strong, standard benchmark: if your defense survives PGD, that means something.

What the attacker is allowed to know

Attacks come in flavors depending on the threat model. In a white-box setting the attacker knows the model's architecture and weights and can compute gradients directly. In a black-box setting they only get to query the model and observe outputs, yet attacks still succeed, partly because adversarial examples often transfer between different models trained for the same task. Being clear about the threat model is half of doing this work honestly; a defense that only holds against a weak attacker is not really a defense.

Adversarial training: fighting fire with fire

The most reliable defense we have is conceptually simple: if the model keeps getting fooled by perturbed inputs, train it on perturbed inputs. Adversarial training generates attacks on the fly during training and forces the model to classify those hard examples correctly.

Formally it becomes a two-player game, a min-max problem. An inner process tries to find the worst perturbation that maximizes the loss; the outer process updates the weights to minimize the loss even against that worst case:

minimize over the weights, the loss of the maximally perturbed input. Train against your strongest imagined adversary, and you become robust to the weaker real ones.

In practice you generate a PGD attack on each batch, then take your gradient step on those adversarial inputs instead of the clean ones. The model slowly learns smoother, wider margins around its decisions.

The price of robustness

Here is the catch that keeps the field busy. Adversarial training is expensive and it is not free in accuracy either.

  • Compute cost. Generating a multi-step attack for every batch can make training several times slower than normal. You are running an optimization loop inside your optimization loop.
  • The robustness-accuracy trade-off. Models hardened against attacks often give up a little accuracy on clean, untouched data. Robustness and raw accuracy pull in different directions, and you have to decide how to balance them.
  • It is attack-specific. Train against one kind of perturbation and a cleverer or different attack may still slip through. Robustness is a direction, not a finish line.

Robustness in a distributed world

Now combine this with everything that makes federated learning hard, and you get the problem I actually work on. Federated adversarial training asks clients to harden the shared model against attacks using only their own local data, then combine those robust updates, all while clients differ in data, in compute, and in how much battery they can spend on those expensive inner attack loops.

That last point matters more than it first appears. A multi-step attack on every batch is a heavy ask for a phone or an edge device. So a realistic system has to be resource-aware: spend the robustness budget where it counts, lean on cheaper approximations elsewhere, and let the aggregator account for the fact that different clients contributed different amounts of robustness. Getting that balance right is, to me, where adversarial machine learning stops being a clean benchmark and starts being engineering.

Why it is worth chasing

Adversarial examples are more than a security nuisance. They are a window into the difference between a model that memorized a surface and a model that understood a structure. Every step toward genuine robustness tends to make models more reliable, more interpretable, and more trustworthy in the places where it actually matters, which is exactly why I keep coming back to it.
